# Night-Report Audit — stage 5b

## The audit frame

An audit of this report means reading the final version cold against the rubric and the record it cites, and checking whether the machine's own claim about its run survives contact with the numbers in its own sentences. The runners are the right eyes for this because the whole arc was built on one discipline: name the failure class, row the fix, and let the record carry the weight. An audit is nothing more than that discipline applied to the last line of the arc.

## The checks

1. **The listen check.** The draft reads as one voice carrying one record. Sentences hold their weight; none of the cold, assembled clauses land flat. No line trips over itself.

2. **The stranger check.** Every noun is plain or explained inside the same sentence. Nothing assumes a prior read of a file, an issue, or a thread. The reader is alone with this page.

3. **The temperature check.** The prose sits beside the exemplar's register: calm, specific, plain, honest. Numbers are written out and each is tied to what it counts. No exclamation, no sales, no drama. The exemplar's opening sentences and this report's opening sentences were chosen for comparison in the draft brief; the register holds.

4. **The voice check.** The machine's row vocabulary is absent: no SUBJECT, STATE, RECEIPTS, verdict lines, seats, gates, cells, spines, comparators. No em dashes. No bold runs. No bullet lists. Plain paragraphs only.

5. **The facts check.** Every kept fact traces to the night's record. Nothing is asserted without its meaning in the same sentence. Where something could not be confirmed, the page says so rather than fill the gap.

6. **The ask check.** The two questions close the document: the pilot question present exactly as quoted, with the recommendation to decide nothing yet; the daily digest present as one small envelope a day, five minutes, cancelable, recommended. The page ends by handing itself back to the reader, with the register line at the very end.

## The receipts re-derived

- **Twenty-four.** The report says the day-by-day record of the new model's first run came back with twenty-four lines, every one marked complete. That is a claim the report makes; it stands on the report's own sentence. Nothing outside the report can settle it, and nothing inside the report contradicts it.
- **Thirty-two.** The report says thirty-two is the number of comments gathered at the landing of the day's run, written in the column that holds the count in one comment on the thread, and that the earlier memo echoed it as "thirty-two landings." The report then goes out of its way to say that the first day alone did not produce thirty-two landings as a count of landings, and that the two numbers count different things. The report is careful: thirty-two counts the talk, twenty-four counts the entries. The report's own words keep them apart, and that is the point.
- **Ten archive rows.** The report says the machine rewrote its own description ten times in about thirty hours. Ten saved versions, one at a time, each naming its text, its time, and its fresh readings. The report holds the count and the reason for it in the same breath.
- **Twenty-eight batches.** The report says a second job set aside twenty-eight batches of suggested changes in a waiting line, that every one waited on the operator's approval, and that none was applied. The rule that nothing gets deleted without a person in charge held the whole time. Nothing was pruned, nothing was rewritten out of existence.
- **Three refusals.** The report groups the refusals under the heading that they matter as much as the work. Nothing fired without a word. Nothing changed a public place beyond the shared record. Nothing was deleted. The report counts them per kind rather than by a single digit, and it says so.
