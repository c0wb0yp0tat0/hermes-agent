## Thesis
A one-lecture overview of how modern LLMs are actually built and how they work. The speaker frames training around five components — architecture, training loss/algorithm, data, evaluation, and systems — and argues that in practice the last three dominate: academia over-focuses on architecture while "what matters in practice is mostly the three other topics, so data, evaluation and systems." The lecture then covers the two training paradigms: pre-training (modeling the internet) and post-training (turning models into AI assistants).

## Section-by-section (caption timestamps)
- **0:00–2:53 — Intro & the five components.** LLMs as chat bots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama); architecture, training loss/algorithm, data, evaluation, systems; Transformers skipped (covered in a prior lecture in the series).
- **2:53–6:33 — Language modeling recap.** LMs as probability distributions over token sequences ("the mouse ate the cheese" vs. "the cheese ate the mouse" — syntactic and semantic knowledge); generative models; autoregressive decomposition via the chain rule.
- **6:33–10:41 — The autoregressive task.** Tokenize → embed → network → linear layer to vocabulary size → softmax; predict next token with cross-entropy, equivalent to maximizing log-likelihood. Output dimensionality equals vocabulary size, so tokenization design matters.
- **10:41–19:06 — Tokenizers.** Why tokens (typos, languages without spaces, quadratic sequence cost of characters); BPE walkthrough (merge most frequent pairs; keep the small sub-tokens); pre-tokenizers as a speed trick; downsides — numbers get opaque tokens ("327 might have its own token"), GPT-4 changed code tokenization; hope to eventually move away from tokenizers.
- **19:06–28:32 — Evaluation.** Perplexity = exponentiated average per-token loss, between 1 and vocab size; fell from ~70 to <10 on a standard set 2017–2023; dropped from academic benchmarking (tokenizer-dependent) but still central to training development; aggregated benchmarks (Stanford HELM, HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard) and MMLU (likelihood of the correct A–D choice); inconsistencies (LLaMA 65B: 63.7 on HELM vs. 48.8 on another harness); train/test contamination and the "generate in order vs. shuffled order" detection trick.
- **28:32–40:39 — Data.** "All of the internet" = Common Crawl (≈250 billion pages, ≈1 petabyte); pipeline: HTML text extraction (math is hard, boilerplate), undesirable-content filtering (NSFW, PII, blacklists), deduplication, heuristic filtering, model-based filtering (classifier trained on Wikipedia-linked pages), domain classification with up/down-weighting (code upweighted because it "helps reasoning," books up, entertainment down), finishing on high-quality data with a decaying learning rate ("you basically overfit on Wikipedia"); scale grew ≈150B tokens (≈800 GB) → ≈15T tokens (Llama 2: 2T, Llama 3: 15T, GPT-4 ≈13T "from leaks"); The Pile composition; ≈15 of Llama's ≈70-person team work on data; companies stay secretive for competitive and copyright reasons.
- **40:39–52:50 — Scaling laws.** Unlike classical ML, larger models + more data never overfit; performance is log-log linear in compute, data, and parameters (OpenAI "Scaling Laws," 2020), so future loss is predictable. New workflow: tune hyperparameters on small models, fit the law, extrapolate, train the final model once (27 days instead of a 1-day best-of-30 sweep); slope vs. intercept — minor architecture changes mostly shift the intercept, data quality shifts the slope; Transformer vs. LSTM example; Chinchilla isoFLOP analysis: ≈20 tokens per parameter is training-optimal, ≈150:1 when inference cost matters.
- **52:50–59:31 — The "bit lesson" & cost.** Richard Sutton's 2019 post: with scaling laws and Moore's law, what matters is architectures that leverage compute — "systems, data, and less so the architecture." Back-of-envelope for Llama 3 400B: ≈3.8×10²⁵ FLOPs (6·params·tokens rule), ≈16,000 H100s, ≈70 days / ≈26–30M GPU-hours, ≈$52M GPU rental, ≈$75M all-in, ≈4,000 t CO₂-eq (≈2,000 JFK–London round trips); a US executive order flags training above 10²⁶ FLOPs (Llama 3 stayed just below); ≈10× FLOPs per model generation.
- **59:31–1:02:27 — Why post-training.** A pure LM (GPT-3) completes "explain the moon landing to a six-year-old" with another question, not an answer; alignment = following instructions and designer desires (e.g., refusing toxic requests); desired data is scarce/expensive while pre-training data is abundant but not what you want.
- **1:02:27–1:09:51 — SFT.** Supervised fine-tuning = same next-token loss on human-written desired answers (OpenAssistant example); "the key of ChatGPT" behind the GPT-3 → ChatGPT jump; Alpaca: 175 human Q&As → 52,000 text-davinci-003-generated Q&As → SFT of LLaMA 7B; LIMA: scaling SFT data 2,000 → 32,000 barely helps because SFT teaches format/specialization, not knowledge ("the knowledge is already in the pre-trained LLM"); human-in-the-loop synthetic data; post-training uses different data and different hyperparameters (higher learning rate, ~1e-5).
- **1:09:51–1:22:55 — RLHF.** SFT problems: behavioral cloning is bounded by human generation ability; a hallucination hypothesis (SFT can teach the model to produce plausible-sounding things it never saw, e.g., a made-up reference); expensive ideal answers. RLHF: generate two answers per instruction, labelers pick the preferred; binary reward is sparse → train a reward model (a Bradley–Terry classifier; softmax logits give a continuous signal); PPO with KL regularization against "over-optimization"; after RL the LM is a *policy* — likelihoods are no longer meaningful. ChatGPT's original recipe: SFT → reward model → iterative PPO. PPO is messy in practice, motivating DPO (Stanford, ~1 year prior): directly maximize likelihood of preferred / minimize dispreferred outputs, same global optimum as PPO under assumptions, simpler, performs as well — now standard in open-source and much of industry. AlpacaFarm: SFT improves, PPO ≈ DPO.
- **1:22:55–1:28:38 — Preference data.** Human labeling is slow, expensive, and hard: humans self-agree only ~66% on binary preference tasks (the five paper authors got ~67–68% after three hours of guideline discussion); humans fixate on surface features — RLHF makes outputs longer; annotator distribution shift; crowdsourcing ethics. AlpacaFarm alternative: LLM preferences are ~50× cheaper than ~$300/1,000 Mechanical Turk labels and agree with the human majority better than humans do — now standard practice.
- **1:28:38–1:33:46 — Evaluating aligned models.** Validation loss is incomparable across methods (PPO vs. DPO) and perplexity is invalid (models are uncalibrated policies); outputs are open-ended, so evaluation uses pairwise human preference: Chatbot Arena (blind votes from hundreds of thousands of users; tech-savvy bias; cost/speed), or LLM-as-judge: AlpacaEval reports GPT-4-judged win rates with 98% correlation to Chatbot Arena in <3 minutes and <$10; length bias is severe — GPT-4 vs. itself is 50%, "be verbose" → 64.4%, "be concise" → 20%; regression controlling for length largely fixes it.
- **1:33:46–1:37:01 — Q&A.** Industry fine-tunes all weights (LoRA is the open-source variant, adding per-layer differences); RLHF data ~1M examples vs. SFT ~5–50k; pre-training is best viewed as weight initialization; what matters is the effective learning rate (Alpaca used 3 epochs).
- **1:37:01–1:43:06 — Systems.** Compute is the bottleneck (GPUs scarce, inter-GPU communication slow); GPUs are throughput-optimized with many cores — matrix multiplication is ~10× faster than anything else; memory/communication lag leaves GPUs idle; MFU (model FLOP utilization) ~50% is "very happy" (LLaMA ≈45%); low precision: 16-bit computation with 32-bit master weights (automatic mixed precision); operator fusion: fused kernels move data once instead of per line — torch.compile rewrites PyTorch to C++/CUDA and gives ~2× speedup. Tiling, parallelization, and mixture of experts are skipped for time.
- **1:43:06–1:44:25 — Closing.** Uncovered topics (architectures, inference, UI, multimodality, data scarcity, legality of data collection); course recommendations: CS224N, CS324, and CS336 ("LLM from scratch… given by my two supervisors… very heavy workload, so be careful").

## Key concepts & techniques taught
Autoregressive next-token prediction with cross-entropy; BPE tokenization and pre-tokenizers; perplexity and benchmark aggregation (HELM, MMLU) with contamination concerns; the full data pipeline (crawl → extract → filter → dedup → domain mixing); scaling laws and Chinchilla-style compute allocation (6·params·tokens FLOP estimate; MFU); SFT and synthetic data (Alpaca, LIMA); RLHF (reward modeling / Bradley–Terry, PPO + KL, DPO); pairwise preference evaluation (Chatbot Arena, AlpacaEval, length-bias control); systems techniques (mixed precision, operator fusion / torch.compile).

## Takeaways a viewer should be able to state
1. An LLM is a probability distribution over token sequences, trained to predict the next token; pre-training models the internet, post-training turns the model into an AI assistant.
2. Data, evaluation, and systems matter more in practice than architecture tweaks — most minor architecture differences only shift a scaling law's intercept.
3. Scaling laws are empirically log-log linear: loss is predictable from compute, data, and parameters; Chinchilla's ≈20 tokens/parameter is training-optimal (~150:1 when inference cost counts).
4. SFT teaches format, not knowledge — a few thousand good examples suffice; RLHF (reward model + PPO, or the simpler DPO) aligns outputs to human preference.
5. After RLHF a model is a policy, so perplexity and validation loss stop being valid evaluation metrics — use human or LLM-judge pairwise comparisons (Chatbot Arena, AlpacaEval), and watch length bias.
6. Training efficiency is dominated by systems: GPUs idle on communication, so 16-bit/32-bit mixed precision and operator fusion (torch.compile, ~2×) are routine wins.
7. Human preference data is noisy (humans self-agree ~66%); LLM-generated preferences are now ~50× cheaper with equal-or-better agreement.
8. A frontier model training run costs on the order of $75M and ~26–30M GPU-hours (Llama 3 400B back-of-envelope), with ~10× compute per model generation.
