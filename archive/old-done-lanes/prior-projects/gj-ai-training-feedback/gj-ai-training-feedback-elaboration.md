# Feedback on the "Responsible AI Use" Training — Elaboration

*Prepared for the AI Council lead · 12 August 2026*

*This is the expanded version of the single-paragraph feedback, ready in case the council asks for detail. The training was produced in-house with the approved AI tools, so every item below is fixable quickly by the same team; and because the training goes to every employee, the items here are the ones that matter at that scale.*

---

## 1. The core feedback: the course does not model what it teaches

The course's message is accountability: "You are responsible for verifying accuracy, tone and appropriateness. You are also accountable for the final outcome." It teaches employees to review, verify, and disclose. But the course itself — produced with AI tools — discloses nothing and shows no sign of the review it preaches. Employees will notice, because the course trains them to notice exactly this. The fix is one sentence at the start of the course: *"This course was produced with the approved AI tools and reviewed by people."* It costs nothing, models the transparency the course demands, and is the strongest defense if anyone asks whether the content was human-reviewed.

## 2. What holds up — do not change

1. **The accountability spine** — "AI assists you remain accountable" and "AI handles speed. You handle judgment." Repeated consistently; the most important message in the course.
2. **"It predicts. It does not think."** Accurate, and it pre-teaches the failure mode: "it can sound confident even when it's wrong."
3. **The approval-status rule** — "If a tool has not been formally approved, it is considered non approved even if it is free, public, widely used, or not blocked." Closes the loopholes most corporate trainings miss.
4. **The rule of thumb** — "If you would not post it publicly, do not enter it into a non approved AI tool."
5. **The escalation path and tone** — "stop, don't proceed, ask" with "this isn't about gotchas, it's about protecting our people, our customers and our company."

## 3. What is outdated as of 12 August 2026

### 3.1 The history anchor is checkable and imprecise
The course dates generative AI's arrival to "the 2000 and 20s." The public record: ChatGPT launched **30 November 2022** — one million users in five days, one hundred million in two months. Fix: one accurate sentence — "In late 2022, ChatGPT made generative AI something anyone could type into. That direct access is what changed responsibility."

### 3.2 No disclosure rule for AI-generated content
The review checklist is "accuracy, tone and appropriateness." It never tells employees to disclose AI-generated content. Where the pressure comes from:
- **Client contracts** increasingly require disclosure of AI-generated content in deliverables.
- **Law, where in scope** — the EU AI Act (Article 50, effective 2 August 2026) requires deployers to disclose deepfakes and AI-generated text published on matters of public interest; the EU rules reach companies only where their AI outputs are used in the EU. California's provider-side transparency law (SB 942) binds AI platforms, not companies that use them.
- **Standard practice** — labeling AI-generated content in client- and customer-facing materials is now the norm.

Fix: one guardrail line — "If content will be seen by customers, clients, or the public, and it was generated or substantially changed by AI, say so — label it" — plus Resource Center guidance on what to label and how.

### 3.3 "You type into it" vs. the agents of 2026
The course frames AI as something you type into. In 2026, agents act: they draft, send, update records, execute workflows. Okta's 2026 survey: 92% of executives report autonomous AI agents in widespread or moderate use — and its definition of agents explicitly includes Microsoft Copilot, the tool the course names. The accountability message survives but must extend: an AI that acts on your behalf is still your action; you verify what it did before it ships.

### 3.4 No version, no refresh cadence
The course has no version stamp and no stated review cycle, and it is already stale in two places (3.1, 3.2). Tools, features, and contracts change faster than annual training cycles. Fix: version + effective date on the course; annual review; a five-minute "what changed" update whenever the council approves a tool, changes policy, or the regulatory summary changes; retire superseded versions.

## 4. Also worth doing

1. **Managers are the escalation path but are not enabled.** "Ask your manager" is the course's first line, and nothing prepares managers to answer. Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index (20,000 workers, ten markets): when managers model AI use, employees report a **17-point lift** in AI impact. Fix: a five-minute manager add-on or one-page manager FAQ.
2. **Bias.** Federal EEOC guidance applies to AI in hiring for every employer; state automated-decisionmaking laws follow the affected person, so G&J is in scope in states where its employees live (e.g., California, Colorado) even with an Ohio/Kentucky base. One line: "AI can reflect bias in its training data; if AI output would feed a decision about a person, check it for fairness and keep the reasoning."
