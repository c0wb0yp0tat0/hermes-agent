# An Update on What the News Is Missing

*Companion piece to "The News Is Missing You." Facts first, then the misconstrue. Every number below was re-checked against primary sources (CDC, FDA, FBI, CISA, ITRC, FAO, IEA, PJM, Nature, Annals of Internal Medicine, Merck, Reuters) in August 2026; where a figure is carried from a secondary summary, it is attributed as such. The reader is still not under-informed by accident. Here is what changed, and what did not.*

---

**Let me be clear about what this is.** The last essay told you what the news was not telling you, and it closed with an offer: read the primary sources, the floor holds. This piece is the follow-through. It is not a new list of grievances in the same shape; it is a check-up on the first essay's claims, plus the new material that landed after it was written. Where the first essay's numbers moved, I give you the new numbers and what moved them. Where the pattern kept going, I show you the pattern continuing. And where the first essay's biggest claim was never actually measured, I am going to say that plainly, because the whole method of these essays is that you get the facts straight first.

The facts first. Then the misconstrue.

## The Number Is Still a Floor — and Now It Is the Largest on Record

The first essay told you about Cyclospora: a parasite most people had never heard of, fifteen thousand-plus Americans sickened, an outbreak traced to iceberg lettuce from a single grower in Mexico, and a number that was a floor, because routine stool tests miss Cyclospora.

The update: the number kept rising, and the outbreak is now formally described as the largest Cyclospora outbreak on record in the United States. As of the FDA's August 20 update, the outbreak tied to Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce stands at **10,930 illnesses across 17 states, 454 hospitalizations, and 2 deaths** — both deaths in Michigan, both with illness onset before the recall. Michigan's own weekly count reached **14,277 cases and 335 hospitalizations** among its residents. The national surveillance number for the season, which includes cases outside this outbreak, sits well above fifteen thousand and still climbing. Recall that 1,180 cases all last season. The floor holds, and the floor is high.

Two things happened since the first essay that matter more than the count.

First, the investigation is still open, and the FDA has now done something it had not done as of the first essay: **begun on-site inspections and sampling at the iceberg lettuce growers in Mexico, in coordination with Mexican officials.** The traceback had converged on Taylor Farms de Mexico in Guanajuato; the epidemiology pointed there; no product sample ever tested positive (the one positive border sample was declared a false positive on July 19). Now the verification is physically traveling to the farm. That is the difference between a recall and an answer.

Second, the shape of the story did not change. The chain — Taco Bell — was the largest pipe, not the culprit. The grower is the node. And a recall is still how the story is told, outbreak-page by outbreak-page, while the systemic question — how a single Mexican produce supplier became the recurring source of a record-breaking outbreak — is not asked in the same breath.

Confidence: High — CDC/FDA primary, updated August 20, 2026. What would change it: formal source attribution; the final case count once the investigation closes.

## The Locks Are Being Changed — and They Are Being Changed More Often Than We Thought

The first essay told you about water utilities being locked out of their own systems: malicious actors changing passwords and IP addresses on internet-facing Rockwell/Allen-Bradley PLCs, seven states since July 27, a quiet and dangerous form of sabotage — loss of view, and in some cases function.

The update: the wave was bigger than the first essay's seven states suggested, and it is not over.

Independent analysis now puts the Minnesota disruption alone at **more than 30 community water systems over a single weekend** — with activity reaching at least seven states including Michigan. CISA, in its July 30 advisory, identified not just Rockwell MicroLogix 1400 PLCs but also **Siemens and Schneider Electric devices**, with thousands of each brand reachable from the public internet. Utilities were forced to switch to manual operation; some issued boil-water notices as a precaution. No confirmed contamination has been reported, and Michigan officials said their systems operated safely — which is the good news, and also the point: the attack succeeded at the level of control before it could be aimed at water quality.

Attribution remains formally open. The FBI and EPA have not assigned the wave to a specific actor. Researchers note patterns consistent with Iran-nexus activity, echoing the 2023 attack on the Municipal Water Authority of Aliquippa, which CISA tied to the IRGC-linked CyberAv3ngers. That is an unconfirmed pattern, not a finding — say so.

The method is the story, and the method is nearly primitive: a controller answering on the open internet with a weak or default password is a controller anyone can command. The same class of access that hit one Pennsylvania booster station in 2023 was, in 2026, spread across seven states in a matter of days. Seven states in four days is a pace, not a conclusion.

Confidence: High on the incidents — FBI/EPA/CISA primary (July 30, 2026), corroborated by sector reporting. What would change it: formal attribution; whether the device classes expand further.

## Seven Recalls Are Still a Pattern — and the List Holds

The first essay listed seven manufacturers who recalled injectable drugs for foreign material — glass, steel, cardboard, cotton fibers — in about two months, and argued that seven recalls are a pattern, not isolated fillers.

The update: the pattern held. The FDA's drug-recall page as of mid-August still carries the same class of entries: **Fresenius Kabi Tyenne (tocilizumab) recalled for glass particles; Baxter 70% Dextrose for stainless steel particulate; American Regent Papaverine for glass and/or paraformaldehyde** — the same quiet Class II notices, the same substance: particulate injected into patients. Nothing in this update is a headline; that is exactly the point the first essay made, and it survives the re-check intact.

Confidence: High — FDA primary. What would change it: a systemic finding by FDA about the injectable supply chain (not yet established — the "alternative" is still the whole point).

## The Wave That Landed After the Essay: Two Produce Outbreaks at Once

This is new material, not an update: the pattern the first essay proved for Cyclospora — one grower, one import corridor, verification failing at the border and at the point of repackaging — happened **again, twice, in the same week**, in the weeks after the essay was written.

**The jalapeño outbreak.** Salmonella Javiana linked to jalapeño peppers grown in Sinaloa, Mexico, and distributed by Coast Citrus Distributors. As of the CDC's update: **431 cases, 32 states, 57 hospitalizations, 0 deaths** — 86 new cases in the latest update, and 91% of interviewed patients reported dining at the same restaurants. Traceback and epidemiology confirmed the source. The recall is scattered across restaurants and repackaged products; the FDA lists them on a Major Product Recalls page.

**The sprouts outbreak.** The same week, a single brand of alfalfa sprouts — Everything Sprouts, LLC, sold under the Calco and Everything Sprouts brands — was linked to a **two-pathogen outbreak**: 55 cases across 15 states, 4 hospitalizations, and — the detail that should stop you — **two people infected with both E. coli and Salmonella at the same time** (46 with E. coli, 7 with Salmonella, 2 with both). The FDA identified multiple E. coli serotypes (O26:H11, O103:H25, O168:H8) plus Salmonella Agona. Recall August 22.

Read those two next to the Cyclospora outbreak and the first essay's claim stops being a claim: it is a documented, recurring mechanism. In one season, at least three produce outbreaks traced to single growers in one import corridor — Mexico to U.S. fresh produce — were each reported as isolated recall notices. The "one bad farm" explanation is falsified by the recurrence. The verification layer at the grower level is the node, and the recall-notice format never names the node as the story.

Confidence: High — CDC/FDA primary, August 21–24, 2026. What would change it: final source determination; whether additional lots or growers are implicated.

## The Checks That Still Work — and the Headlines That Outrun Them

The first essay was honest about confidence — that was part of its authority. The update gets a whole new act on that theme, because five of the six biggest science stories of the week moved in the same direction: the headline inflated the certainty, and the caveat that would have kept it honest was dropped or buried.

**The shingles vaccine and dementia.** Headline: "Shingrix cuts dementia risk by a quarter." Verified facts: a study of **509,926 Medicare adults** entering skilled-nursing facilities found vaccinated adults had a **24% lower risk of a dementia diagnosis over four years** — but only **8,843 of 509,926 people received the vaccine (1.7%)**, the study is **funded by GlaxoSmithKline, which makes Shingrix**, and it is an observational analysis using target-trial emulation — an association, not causation, as the authors themselves state. The "one in 17 dementia cases prevented" framing is the authors'; the caveat (tiny vaccinated group, funder-made-the-product, association not cause) is what the headline drops. (Annals of Internal Medicine, 2026;179(7):958.)

**The mRNA cancer vaccine.** Headline: "mRNA vaccine stops melanoma." Verified facts: Moderna and Merck's intismeran plus Keytruda **met its primary endpoint (recurrence-free survival) and a key secondary endpoint (distant metastasis-free survival)** in the Phase 3 INTerpath-001 trial — a genuine milestone, the first positive Phase 3 for an mRNA cancer vaccine. And: **overall survival data are not yet available**, detailed data have not been released, and the announcement came from the two companies with the most commercial stake (Moderna shares surged as much as 160%). Interim, headline-only, and real. Both things are true; the "cure" framing races past the missing endpoints.

The pattern is the story: in the same week, two of the biggest science stories arrived with the certainty turned up and the design turned down — a 1.7% vaccinated group, a funder-made-the-product disclosure, a missing survival endpoint. That is not a coincidence of sloppy journalism; it is the format. The announcement document is where the claim is maximized and the design is minimized, and the single-story format strips the caveat that a comparative frame would preserve.

Confidence: High on the figures as reported — Annals of Internal Medicine and Merck press release primary; Medium on any causal claim. What would change it: full data at the medical meeting; trial confirmation.

## The Layer Under the Stories: What Is Actually Verified

The first essay's thesis was that you are under-informed by format — single-story, brand-shaped, image-driven, allergic to counting and to framing shifts. The sequel to that thesis, in the material that landed afterward, is that the pattern is not only in the *coverage*; it is in the *systems themselves*. The verification layer — the layer that checks the hire, the label, the recall, the paper — is failing across domains at once, and each failure is emitted in a format that atomizes it.

What is verified, straight:

- **Hiring.** The FBI confirmed a North Korean remote IT worker inside a U.S. federal agency — hired, not hacked. The ITRC counted **21 malicious-insider events in H1 2026 versus 3 in all of 2025** — a sevenfold increase it calls "never previously documented." The FBI's first-ever AI-fraud category: **22,364 complaints, ~$893 million in losses**.
- **Goods.** The FAO's Technical Paper 742: **~20% of global seafood is mislabeled**, up to 30% at restaurants. The recall audits: **420 recall announcements** covering a home-safety pattern that is only visible when counted (the PIRG "Safe At Home in 2026" count; the "40 million items / 12 deaths" figures from the first draft carry an unresolved discrepancy against other summaries of the same report and should not be repeated until the primary is read).
- **Systems.** PJM capacity prices went from **$28.92 to $329.17 per megawatt-day in two years** — the FERC-approved cap hit. **517 of 809 planned U.S. data centers** sit in drought-affected areas, and data-center water demand is heading toward **73 billion gallons a year by 2028**, up from about 17 billion in 2023. The IEA: China holds **~60% of rare-earth mining and 91% of refined output**, and full implementation of export controls could put **~$6.5 trillion a year** of downstream production outside China at risk. The average American dam is **64 years old**, built for rainfall patterns that no longer exist.
- **The literature.** *Nature*'s headline: "staggering 90% of biomedical papers now show signs of AI help." Headline verified; measure definitional — a preprint's detector of "signs of," not a count of authorship. It is a Medium-confidence figure with a caveat that must ride with it.

Now the honest part, and it is the part the first essay did not measure: **"the news misses it" was never counted.** Nothing in this material quantifies coverage — no story counts, no airtime, no severity-versus-coverage comparison. The compilations are selections of items the analyst already judged under-reported. That is selection by hypothesis, not a test of it. The verification failures are verified; the atomizing formats are documented on at least four items; but the headline claim — that the news is missing these stories *systematically* — requires a measurement it does not yet have.

So this essay says what it can stand on and stops there: the failures are real, recurring, cross-domain, and primary-sourced. The formats that atomize them are documented. Whether the news is missing them *on purpose* is the part that still needs the audit — and the invitation below is how you count it.

## The Pattern Is the Story

The first essay's closing offer was: read the primary sources; the floor holds. The update's closing is the same offer, one layer deeper.

The floor holds because the checks still work — but the checks are the story now, and they are failing at the nodes. The record-breaking Cyclospora outbreak is still open, and the verification is now physically in the Mexican fields. The water locks are being changed across seven states and thirty Minnesota systems, and nobody has said who is turning the keys. The recalls still pile up as Class II notices, one manufacturer at a time. The produce wave came three growers deep in a single season. The science headlines keep outrunning their own caveats, and the literature cannot police itself while 90% of its output shows signs of the thing that wrote this essay's sentences.

You are not under-informed by accident. You are under-informed by format — and the format is now the infrastructure. The checks are the new firewall, and the news reports each failure as a one-off, so the collapse of checking never runs as a story.

Read the primary sources. The floor holds — until the checks stop holding it.
